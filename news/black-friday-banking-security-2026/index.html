Black Friday Banking Safety: Protect Your Money and Accounts

MR Techworks · Published November 27, 2026 · Editorial research checked November 27, 2026 · Originally scheduled for November 26

A bank fraud alert can be real, but an unexpected text or call is not proof that the sender works for your bank. During the holiday shopping rush, slow down before responding. Scammers may claim your card was used, ask for a sign-in code, or insist that you move money to a new “safe” account.

The fake fraud-department call

The Federal Trade Commission describes scammers who impersonate bank fraud teams. A message may ask whether you authorized a large purchase; once you reply, someone calls and sounds like an employee. They might know your name or display your bank’s caller ID. Neither proves the caller is genuine. The FDIC separately warns about banking lookalike websites, fraudulent apps and impersonation by text, email and phone.

Safe response: End the conversation. Open your bank’s app from your own phone, or call the number printed on your payment card. Do not follow the caller’s links or call a number supplied in the suspicious message. Ask your bank to verify whether it really issued a fraud alert.

Three requests that should stop the conversation

  1. “Read me the security code we just sent.” That one-time code helps authenticate a login or transaction. Never tell it to someone who contacted you.
  2. “Move your balance to our protected account.” The FTC is explicit: moving money to protect it is a scam. Banks do not require you to send your savings to an unknown destination for safety.
  3. “Install this remote-support app.” A stranger who requests remote access may be trying to view banking information or control your device. Refuse and contact your bank independently.

Before Black Friday or Cyber Monday checkout

  1. Use a unique password for your bank and email; store strong passwords in a reputable password manager.
  2. Turn on multifactor authentication or passkeys when supported. Never share verification codes, even with someone claiming to be security staff.
  3. Enable transaction alerts and review recent activity in your bank’s official app. An alert does not replace checking statements.
  4. When buying online, compare the seller and payment method. A credit card generally offers stronger dispute protections for shopping than irreversible payment methods.
  5. Keep your phone, browser and financial apps updated. Download banking apps through the institution’s official website or your device’s trusted app store.

Already responded or sent money?

Contact your bank’s fraud department immediately using a trusted number. Explain what information you shared, whether you authorized a transfer, and whether someone gained remote access. Ask the bank about securing accounts, reversing or disputing transactions when possible, and replacing compromised credentials. Change affected passwords from a trusted device, review linked accounts and report suspected fraud through ReportFraud.ftc.gov. Recovery is not guaranteed; acting quickly matters.
